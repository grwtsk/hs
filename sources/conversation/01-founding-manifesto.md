# Noeaaeue Hs
 
**The interface belongs to the person using it.**

It does not belong to a design system, a recommendation engine, a standard, a population average, or the institution that delivers it.

Those things may inform an interface. They may not acquire the person’s agency.

Noeaaeue begins with a human behind a user agent. The user agent carries that human’s instructions, permissions, and chosen limits. The presentation exists to honor that relationship—not to substitute an interpretation of the person for the person themselves.

## I. Begin with almost nothing

At rest, grwtsk.com presents a single pictorial label at the bottom of the page.

That label is the supplied mark. It identifies the attached project through the presentation the person has authorized. It is not an advertisement for the interface, an invitation to configure everything, or a place to accumulate controls.

There is no visible page text in this initial state. No introduction. No instruction demanding attention. No decorative navigation. No unsolicited explanation of what the person might become by interacting.

The resting image occupies **144 × 144 pixels**, contained within softly continuous rounded corners. Their geometry comes from an apple, the fruit: the way a body can be round without being a perfect sphere, with fullness and curvature that vary without becoming abrupt.

Abstract that quality into the corners. The reference is an organic form, not Apple Computer, a branded design language, or a borrowed convention.

The image requires no border. Its surrounding field remains nearly transparent: the faint, approximately one-percent veil of the supplied image, not an opaque tile.

The initial visual contract is already present in that image: its light tone, its dark tone, and the density expressed by their proportions.

Do not replace these with fashionable defaults. Do not reinterpret them through an inferred personality, a demographic category, or the preferences of the designer.

A preference the person has already expressed or deliberately delegated through their user agent is not a default. It is an instruction.

Where there is no instruction, begin with the supplied image.

## II. Be silent until chosen

Presence is not permission.

A page visit is not permission to animate. A pointer passing over the mark is not permission to personalize. Time spent looking is not permission to infer an intention. A cache entry is not permission to remember more.

The interface remains quiet until the person chooses to act.

**Clicking and holding turns the mark from a presentation into an instrument.** Movement during that deliberate engagement expresses a choice. Release ends the manipulation.

The gesture must not become a test of dexterity. An equivalent action expressed through the person’s keyboard, assistive technology, or other authorized input must carry the same agency.

Silence does not mean withholding access. The mark must be intelligible through the user agent without covering the page in instructions the person did not request. Its purpose, available actions, and current state must remain accessible without requiring sight, a pointer, or the perception of motion.

The system must distinguish between making a choice available and demanding that someone make it.

## III. Give movement a stable meaning

The shape is not a button hiding a settings dashboard. It is the geometry of the settings themselves.

Angular movement is interpreted at the specified scale of **\(1/(2\pi)\) radians**. Each such movement must have a coherent meaning within the continuous geometry. Its effect must not be secretly reassigned according to popularity, predicted engagement, or an experiment.

**The vertical controls volume: the sphere’s own volume and the volume of the presentation it carries.**

The sphere responds as one body. Its light and dark portions expand or contract together; the phase determines their relative proportions. Vertical movement must not secretly become another density control.

Volume governs extent. Density governs demand. A change in volume does not authorize the system to increase density, fetch additional material, or exceed the person’s permissions.

The resting dimensions establish the starting state. A change in volume follows a deliberate choice, not an idle animation, a passing pointer, or an inference about attention.

The regions of the sphere identify the other domains of choice.

**Below the equator is typography.** This includes typeface and the conditions under which text becomes readable: size, spacing, measure, weight, and rhythm. Typography exists as a capability before text is present. There is no need to place sample words on the resting page merely to prove that the capability exists.

**At the equator is density.** Density governs arrangement, exposure, and the amount of interpretation the interface asks of the person. It concerns both the design and the content—not merely the distance between boxes.

**Above the equator is color.** Color is selected through the image itself, within a palette whose permitted relationships preserve accessibility.

The vertical, the angular movement, and the region being engaged must remain distinguishable in their consequences. The same gesture must not acquire an undisclosed second meaning.

The image is both the instrument and the immediate account of its state. It should not require a second interface to explain what the first interface has done.

The person learns a relationship between movement and consequence. The system must keep that relationship trustworthy.

## IV. Treat density as the whole burden

The moonlike phase represents density.

In the initial presentation, the light portion represents the background: space in which the person is not being asked to distinguish, interpret, decide, or act. The dark portion represents the foreground: the demands made by both the presentation and what it presents.

This is not merely a ratio of painted pixels.

A visually sparse page can still demand too much. A short sentence can carry more interpretive burden than a large image. A hidden control can impose a cost through the effort required to discover it.

The foreground must therefore stand for the **combined burden of design and content**.

As the phase changes, the interface changes its demand. Layout, grouping, simultaneous choices, explanatory depth, and the amount of content exposed at once must answer to that choice together.

Reducing density must not mean quietly deleting qualifications, concealing uncertainty, or turning a difficult claim into a reassuring falsehood. It may mean staging information, changing its organization, or allowing the person to approach it at a different pace.

Increasing density must not mean adding decoration to make the page look more sophisticated.

Changing volume must not evade the density choice. A larger presentation is not permission to fill every available space. A smaller presentation is not permission to make essential information unreachable.

The phase is a design budget, not a claim that the system has measured a mind. The person determines the demand they are prepared to accept.

## V. Make accessibility a property of the material

Color must not move freely into failure and then receive a warning.

The permitted color space must behave as though its boundaries are part of the material.

As a gesture approaches a boundary where foreground and background would lose their necessary distinction, the image resists. Further movement produces less color change and more visible tension in the shape.

The person encounters the limit through the behavior of the object, not through a reprimand.

The constraint is not a prohibition on gray. The initial image itself uses neutral tones. The prohibition is on allowing foreground and background to collapse into an unusable relationship.

**Do not offer an inaccessible state and call the opportunity to select it agency.**

Accessibility constrains what the system offers. It does not grant the system permission to erase choices the person has deliberately expressed through their user agent. A standard may provide evidence for a constraint; it may not impersonate the person.

The rendering must preserve distinction in the context where it is used, including when text becomes visible. An attractive palette preview is not sufficient if the resulting content cannot be read.

Typography, volume, and density must honor the same obligation. A choice must not make the instrument impossible to operate or the information impossible to reach.

Accessibility is not a separate mode attached after the object has been designed. It belongs to what the object can become.

## VI. Let resistance deform the shape, not the contract

Resistance should be felt through animation principles: easing, compression, restrained deformation, and recovery.

As the accessible boundary approaches, the shape may appear to gather tension. It may yield less to the same movement. When the person retreats or releases, it settles without crossing the boundary it was protecting.

There is no need for a warning flash, a vibrating control, or an elastic performance that competes with the content.

The material may deform. The accessibility constraint may not.

All transitions should share one perceptual language: **the ball is turning**.

This does not require the body to become a mathematically perfect sphere. Its organic fullness and softly continuous corners remain part of its character. Turning, changing volume, and yielding under resistance are behaviors of the same object—not excuses to replace it with another.

The movement is ordinarily almost invisible. There is no idle spinning. No perpetual demonstration. No ornamental motion intended to keep the person looking. Outside a light–dark reversal, deformation under resistance should provide the principal hint of movement.

WebGL2 is the intended rendering medium for this behavior. It is not the source of its legitimacy. A fallback must preserve the same agency, palette constraints, and state, rather than replace them with a different interaction.

Where the user agent conveys a choice to reduce motion, that choice governs the rendering. State and limits must remain understandable without relying on animation.

The metaphor does not outrank the person.

## VII. Change faces without passing through unreadability

In the initial orientation, the light color is the background and the dark color is the foreground.

When the person moves between light and dark presentation, the colors they have accepted exchange roles. The transition should feel like the same object turning to reveal its other face—not like a new theme replacing the old one.

At the point where ordinary interpolation would collapse foreground into background, the presentation must **flip rather than settle into sameness**.

A flip is not a crossfade through illegibility.

The perceived movement may remain continuous while the assignment of foreground and background changes at the turn. No displayed content should be required to pass through an unusable intermediate palette merely to satisfy an animation.

The phase continues to account for demand by role. Foreground remains the demand made by the presentation; background remains the space around that demand. Reversing the colors does not reverse the person’s density choice.

The person keeps their colors. The object changes orientation.

The reversal may be unmistakable. Everything around it should remain restrained.

## VIII. Let expression change; make claims answer to evidence

When text appears, the person may select it and change it.

They may change its typography, wording, organization, and local presentation. They may annotate, question, reinterpret, and propose alternatives. The interface must not treat the person as a passive endpoint for an untouchable publication.

But the authority to change a presentation is not the authority to falsify its evidence.

A change to an evidence-bearing claim must retain its provenance and distinguish a personal rendering, a proposed correction, and an established result. A claim does not become demonstrated because it has been edited into place.

**To replace an established claim as established knowledge, present the objective proof or checkable evidence that warrants the replacement.**

Evidence is not protected from challenge. It is protected from silent substitution.

The original reasoning, the correction, and the grounds for that correction must remain distinguishable. Uncertainty must not disappear because a simpler interface would prefer certainty.

A lower-density presentation may stage the evidence. It may not sever the claim from its support or conceal the existence of a material qualification.

Neither popularity nor proximity can discharge a proof obligation. Neither institutional approval nor a beautiful presentation can make an unsupported statement true.

The person owns their expression. Claims of fact remain accountable to what supports them.

## IX. Let locality suggest, never command

Beyond what is constrained by evidence, the system may offer what is useful, available, locally relevant, or popular within the population represented by a cache.

These are suggestions—not verdicts about the person.

Geography enters the architecture first as a constraint on communication. Distance, finite signal propagation, and the distribution of storage shape where information can be available and how quickly it can arrive.

Locality should inform delivery where it reduces delay. It must not become a shortcut for assigning identity, taste, ability, or belief.

The density of a cache is not the density a person must accept. The volume of available information is not the volume the interface is entitled to expose.

What is nearby is not necessarily correct. What is cached is not necessarily current. What is popular is not necessarily wanted. What is fast to deliver is not necessarily worth presenting.

The user agent mediates what may be fetched, retained, shared, and suggested. Permissions remain attached to the purposes for which the person gave them; they do not expand simply because distribution makes expansion convenient.

A local edit does not silently become a public contribution. A suggestion does not silently become a preference. A remembered state does not silently become permission to profile.

**The cache may suggest. It may not decide.**

## X. Keep the human at the boundary

Every boundary in Noeoueeue must have an accountable purpose.

A color boundary protects distinction. A density boundary protects the person’s chosen level of demand. An evidence boundary protects the difference between assertion and demonstration. A permission boundary protects the human whose agent is acting.

None of these boundaries exists to make the system more important.

The interface must not convert assistance into authority, convenience into consent, or a prediction into an instruction. It must not make its own complexity the person’s responsibility.

Its success is not how much interaction it produces. Its success is how little unwanted work stands between a person and a chosen act.

Begin with the supplied mark.

Let its rounded corners carry the continuity of an apple, not the authority of a brand. Let its vertical govern volume, with light and dark belonging to one body. Let its phase express demand. Let its surface express color. Let its lower geometry govern typography without inventing text to fill the silence.

Let resistance make limits perceptible. Let the turning preserve the person’s choices. Let every equivalent means of action carry the same authority.

Let expression remain editable, evidence remain answerable, and suggestions remain suggestions.
